Physician-Scientist Advising Toolkit

A practical, evidence-based toolkit for MD-PhD students and program directors — to weigh specialties, training pathways, and funding; to search a national directory of MD-PhD programs and residency PSTPs; and to structure honest conversations about building a physician-scientist career.

Built on the physician-scientist training literature — national MD-PhD outcomes (Brass & Akabas, JCI Insight 2019) and ASCI/AAIM physician-scientist development work — plus the public NIH/NIGMS MSTP roster. See the Evidence base under the Advising Toolkit.

A toolkit for advising — and for being advised

Choosing how to build a physician-scientist career is one of the hardest decisions in training, and it depends as much on the institution and pathway as on the specialty itself. This toolkit gives students a structured way to explore options and program directors and mentors a shared reference for advising — grounded in national outcomes data rather than any single program's experience.

Advising Toolkit

A reference for advising conversations: the factors that most shape long-term success, the structured research tracks, the funding mechanisms that protect research time, and where each fits across a career.

Why this matters. Physician-scientists are roughly 1.5% of the U.S. physician workforce, and the pipeline has been aging and contracting (NIH Physician-Scientist Workforce Report, 2014). MD-PhD graduates are about 3% of medical-school graduates yet make up close to half of NIH-funded physician-scientists (AAMC National MD-PhD Program Outcomes Study, 2018). Average time to both degrees rose from ~6.7 years (1975–84) to ~8.25 years (2005–14), and postgraduate training has lengthened as well.

The physician-scientist training arc

From college to research independence, with the funding mechanisms that protect research time at each step. Durations are typical and vary widely by specialty, research scope, and individual path — and postgraduate training has lengthened over recent decades.

Questions to ask any program

A general checklist an advisor can hand a student — for residencies, fellowships, or any physician-scientist training step. It complements the specialty-specific prompts in the Specialty Explorer. (Checkboxes are for working through in the moment and reset on reload.)

Protected research time

  • How much protected research time is guaranteed, and is it in writing?
  • Which research-track or physician-scientist pathway is offered (e.g., ABIM / ABP / Holman, R38 / StARR, institutional T32)?
  • How is research time defended against clinical, call, and service demands?

Mentorship

  • Who would my mentors be, and do they hold active R / K funding?
  • How are mentor matches made, and is there a mentoring committee?
  • Is there a community of physician-scientist peers and near-peer mentors (e.g., PSTP residents)?

Funding & track record

  • What is the recent record of trainees obtaining K-awards (K08 / K23) and moving to R-funding?
  • What bridge or institutional funding exists between awards?
  • Is there support for the Loan Repayment Program (LRP) and grant-writing?

Structure & outcomes

  • What is the typical time to an independent position from here?
  • Where are recent physician-scientist graduates now, and what share stay research-active?
  • How many physician-scientist trainees are enrolled, and what is attrition like?

National Outcomes & Evidence Base

The data behind the toolkit: national MD-PhD outcomes, the published guidance this resource draws on, and where to look up institution-level funding.

National MD-PhD outcomes (Brass & Akabas, JCI Insight 2019). From the national study of 10,591 graduates of 80 MD-PhD programs:
  • ~60% of graduates train in internal medicine, pathology, or pediatrics; another ~25% in neurology, surgery, psychiatry, or radiology.
  • Weighted across responders and non-responders, ~71% work in academia, the NIH, research institutes, or industry; ~24% in private practice.
  • Among full-time academic alumni, 63% hold research grants — rising to 87% for those with ≥50% research effort.
  • Share reporting ≥50% research effort ranges from ~10% (family medicine, orthopedic surgery) to >50% (neurology, internal medicine, pediatrics, pediatric neurology).
  • Anesthesiology had the shortest time to a first academic position; surgery the longest.

Evidence base & further reading

Two databases worth knowing. The Blue Ridge Institute for Medical Research compiles NIH funding by institution, department, and specialty. NIH RePORTER finds T32 grants, R01-funded investigators, and K-to-R transitions at programs of interest.
